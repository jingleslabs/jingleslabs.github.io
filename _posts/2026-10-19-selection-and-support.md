A list of successful alumni leaves an important question unanswered: which parts of a program helped them do something they could not otherwise have done?

That question matters when deciding what to copy. A grant, a community, a mentor and a recognizable credential are different things. Reproducing the visible format of a successful program does not establish that the useful part has been reproduced.

## What the account actually offers

[First Round Review’s retrospective on the Thiel Fellowship](https://review.firstround.com/inside-thiel-fellowship/) describes both a demanding selection process and practical support after admission. Organizers looked for people already showing initiative. The program then offered resources, mentorship and a peer community. Its activities changed in response to participants’ feedback.

The article also records disagreement about success: company outcomes, acceleration of individual paths and broader cultural influence are different proposed measures. Recognition itself appears in participants’ accounts of what helped.

This is a reported history, not a controlled comparison. It offers candidate explanations worth examining. It does not isolate how much of the eventual outcome came from selection, support or the credential.

## Make the explanation specific enough to question

Here is a proposed way to read a program case study. For each feature worth copying, write one sentence explaining how it is supposed to help. Then name an observation that would make that explanation less convincing.

Consider a hypothetical peer workshop for adults building open-source tools. Suppose its organizers believe weekly meetings help participants get unstuck.

“Participants shipped more projects” is an outcome to investigate. It is still missing the link between the meetings and the work. A more specific record would ask:

- What was blocked before a meeting?
- What useful information or help became available there?
- What changed afterward?
- What other help or resources arrived during the same period?
- Did some participants make progress without using the meetings?

These questions do not produce a causal estimate. They make the proposed mechanism easier to inspect, including cases that do not fit the explanation. Collect only information participants agree to share; a useful review need not become intrusive surveillance.

The next decision can then be narrower than whether to copy the whole program. Perhaps the experiment worth trying is access to timely technical feedback. A weekly meeting is one possible way to provide it.

## Decide what counts before choosing the examples

For that hypothetical workshop, I would define the review period and intended benefit before collecting success stories. If the purpose is to resolve technical blockers, record that purpose explicitly. A popular event or a larger network may be valuable, but it should not silently replace the original question.

Include unfinished projects and participants who leave, where they consent to follow-up. Record how many people were invited to respond and how many did. A few vivid accounts can suggest what to examine next; they cannot stand in for the experiences of everyone involved.

Keep the conclusions proportional to the evidence. “Several participants described useful feedback” is a different finding from “the workshop caused more projects to ship.” The former can justify improving a feedback session without pretending to establish the latter.

A good case study can help generate a sharper experiment. The practical output is a feature to try, a reason it might help, and a way to notice when it does not.

*Reading scope: the accessible text of the First Round Review article was read for this note. Photographs and linked articles were not analyzed. The framework and workshop example above are proposed analysis, not results from a program evaluated here.*
